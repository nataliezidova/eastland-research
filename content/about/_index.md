## Flower in the River

*Flower in the River* is a weekly podcast and research project documenting the individuals connected to the 1915 Eastland Disaster in Chicago. The project combines forensic genealogy, archival research, and participatory storytelling to uncover and preserve the stories of those affected by this tragedy.

## About the Researcher

I'm a genealogist, podcaster, and graduate student pursuing Applied Public History at the University of London. My connection to the Eastland Disaster is personal—my great-aunt Martha Pfeiffer died in the tragedy—but my approach is systematic and evidence-based.

My background spans corporate IT, state government, investigative work, and writing instruction, giving me the analytical tools to approach historical research with the rigor it deserves.

## Research Principles

### Evidence-Based Documentation

Every claim on this site is fully cited and sourced. I use forensic documentation tools like Hunchly to create timestamped, legally admissible records. No assertion is made without verification through primary sources.

### Attribution and Transparency

I believe in being a "guardian not gatekeeper" of historical knowledge. This means:

- Always providing full citations
- Preserving attribution for all contributors
- Making research processes transparent
- Never claiming ownership of information that should belong to all

### Continuous Investigation

The Eastland Disaster is not a closed case. Through systematic archival research, I continue to discover new victims, correct errors, and refine our understanding of what happened on July 24, 1915.

## Academic Work

I'm currently writing an article for *The Public Historian* examining how heritage organizations can damage historical knowledge when they claim authority without conducting proper research. This work has received support from prominent genealogists including Elizabeth Shown Mills, Crista Cowan, and Lisa Louise Cooke.

## Preservation

The University of Minnesota has approached me to preserve this research in their archives, recognizing its scholarly value and methodology.
